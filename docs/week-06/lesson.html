Week 06 · Making a set, not seven pictures — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

You have photographs and you have been editing them one at a time. This week they have to become a campaign — and a campaign is not a collection of good images. It is a set that holds together, which is a property none of the images has on its own.

1 · Cleaner is not the same as different

Retouching removes what is in the way. That is worth doing and it is not the outcome.

6.1 asks that the edit changes what the photograph communicates, not only how clean it is. Those are different achievements and only one of them is being assessed.

The edit made it cleaner The edit changed what it says
Removed sensor dust and a bright patch in the corner Darkened the surrounding room so the figure reads as alone rather than as one of a crowd
Corrected a colour cast Warmed the light so the space reads as somewhere you would stay rather than somewhere you would leave
Straightened the horizon Cropped in so the empty chairs are the subject rather than the background
Reduced noise Lifted the shadow on a face so an expression becomes readable

Every left-hand edit is defensible and none of them would satisfy the outcome on its own.

The test is 6.3: what does this image now say that the original did not? If you cannot answer, the edit is technically fine and evidentially empty. And you must be able to answer it against the original, which is why the originals are submitted with the set.

2 · Where retouching stops clarifying

You met this in week 2 as a viewer. This week you are the one deciding, which is harder.

Removing a blemish, removing a bystander, and removing the context that made a subject's situation legible are the same technique with three different relationships to the truth.

The test has not changed: would somebody conclude something from this image that is not true?

Two additional questions belong to you specifically, as somebody photographing real people for a real organization:

  • Would the subject recognise themselves in this? Not "would they like it" — would they say that is what it was like.
  • Would the organization stand behind it if asked how it was made?

If either answer is no, the edit is a problem however skilful it is.

3 · Treatment

A treatment is the consistent visual handling applied across a set — tonal, colour, and textural. It is what makes seven photographs taken in different light on different days read as one piece of work.

A treatment is not a preset. It is a set of decisions applied deliberately and identically:

The decision
Tone How dark the shadows sit, how bright the highlights run, how much contrast
Colour Warm or cool, how saturated, whether one colour is pulled forward
Texture Grain, sharpness, how much fine detail is retained
Crop convention Aspect ratio, how tightly framed, where the subject sits

The last one surprises people. A set where three images are tightly cropped square and four are loose landscape does not read as one campaign no matter how well the tones match.

4 · Consistency is invisible in an image

Here is the structural fact of the week, and it is the reason for how the session is run.

A single photograph cannot be consistent with itself. Consistency is a relationship between images, so it does not exist inside any one of them — and you have spent two weeks looking at your images one at a time, on a laptop, at whatever size fits.

That working method makes each image better and makes the set worse. You correct each photograph toward what it needs, and seven individually excellent images drift into seven different treatments.

You cannot see this happening. Not because you are careless — because the information is not on your screen. It is in the comparison.

The fix is mechanical rather than clever: put every image where you can see them all at once. A wall, a large display, a printed contact sheet. Then work across the set rather than within it.

Working within images Working across the set
Open one, perfect it, close it, open the next All of them visible; adjust toward each other
Each image judged against what it needs Each image judged against the others
Produces seven good photographs Produces one campaign

5 · What a consistency pass actually is

  1. Display everything at once. Not in a folder — visible simultaneously.
  2. Name the three things that drift most. Usually tone, colour temperature, and crop convention.
  3. Pick the target. Not the best image — the one whose treatment you want the rest to move toward.
  4. Move the others toward it. Across the set, not one at a time.
  5. Have somebody else look. Ask them to point at the image that does not belong. If they cannot, the treatment holds.

Step 5 is the same instrument as the reading-order test in week 4, applied to a different question. You cannot see the odd one out in your own set for the same reason you cannot read your own composition: you know what you meant.

Module 4 vocabulary

Term Means
Retouching Editing a photograph so it communicates more clearly than the original did. Not the same as cleaning it
Treatment The consistent visual handling applied across a set — tonal, colour, and textural
Consistency The property of a treatment holding across a set. Cannot be judged within a single image
Unedited original The photograph as captured, submitted untouched alongside the retouched version. Required — it is the comparison retouching is graded against
Before-and-after pair An unedited original shown with its retouched result

Words you will hear but do not own yet

  • Sequence — the order images are arranged in so the set carries what no single image does. Owned and assessed in week 7.
  • Walkthrough — the live account of the campaign. Week 8.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. Name three edits that make an image cleaner without changing what it says.
  2. Why can consistency not be judged from one image, however good it is? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  3. What are the four parts of a treatment, and which one do people forget?
  4. Removing a blemish and removing the context of somebody's situation are the same technique. What separates them?
  5. You have spent two weeks editing on a laptop, one image at a time. What has that been doing to your set? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  6. Why are your unedited originals submitted, and what happens if they are not?

What happens in the session

Somebody's whole set goes on the wall in the first three minutes, and the room decides whether it is one campaign. Then you will see one photograph retouched two ways — both competent, only one changing what it says.

In the lab you run your own consistency pass with everything visible at once, and take part in critique session 3.

Afterwards you submit Phase 3 — working files, exports, and your unedited originals — as the assignment.
