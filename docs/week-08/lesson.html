Week 08 · The campaign, and the account of it — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 15 minutes — but the work attached to it is the largest pre-class ask of the course. Start early.

Last week a stranger told you what they saw in your lead image, and it was probably not what you intended. This week you do something with that, and then you account for the whole thing out loud.

1 · What the test actually told you

A contradicting reading order is not a fault in one photograph. It is information about how you compose — a habit that will be in your other images too, because it is yours.

If viewers went to a bright background instead of your subject, go and look: how many of your other images have a bright area you did not notice because you were looking at the subject? Usually most of them.

8.1 is assessed on whether the revision went across the set. Revising only the image you tested treats the test as being about that photograph, which is the one thing it is not about.

Response What it demonstrates
Fixed the tested image You can take feedback
Fixed the tested image and checked the rest 8.1
Fixed the rest, and left one deliberately because the effect is wanted there 8.1, and you know why

That last row is worth aiming for. Finding an exception and being able to defend it is stronger than applying a change everywhere without thinking.

2 · The revision will break your consistency

Expect this. Your treatment was tuned in week 6, before you changed anything.

If you darken three backgrounds, those three images now sit differently against the four you did not touch. So the final consistency check is not a formality — run it with everything visible at once, exactly as in week 6, after the revision rather than before.

3 · Accounting for the work

The walkthrough is you going through your campaign, image by image: what you intended, what you did, and how it serves the campaign.

It is worth 5% of your campaign, and it is a container — it evidences no learning outcome. Everything it discusses has already been assessed elsewhere. That is deliberate: a confident walkthrough cannot rescue a weak campaign, and the arithmetic makes that true rather than merely stated.

What it does test is 8.2, and 8.2 has a specific shape.

You will be asked about an image you did not plan to dwell on. Every student will be. That is not a trick — it is stated here, a week in advance, precisely so it is not one.

The reason is that a prepared script tells nobody anything. Three well-rehearsed images demonstrate that you can rehearse. Being asked about the fourth one, the one you find least interesting, is the only part of the walkthrough that could not have been written in advance.

So prepare to talk about all of them. For each image, be able to say three things:

  1. What you intended it to do in the campaign
  2. What you did to it — and the originals are submitted, so this is checkable
  3. Why it is in the set at all

Question 3 is the one students have not thought about. If an image is in your campaign only because you took it, say so — that is an honest answer and a better one than a retrofitted justification.

4 · What "structured by the argument" means

The Exceeds descriptor for the walkthrough asks that it be structured by the campaign's argument rather than by the order the images were made.

Most walkthroughs go: here is the first one I shot, here is the second one I shot. That is a production history and the audience has no reason to care about it.

A walkthrough structured by the argument goes: here is what people currently believe, here is the image that contradicts it, here is what happens next. That is your sequence from last week, spoken.

If you built a real sequence in week 7, your walkthrough is already structured. Use it.

5 · Vocabulary

No new terms this week. Everything you need is already yours, from all four modules — and using it is part of what a walkthrough demonstrates.

Worth going back to the Glossary before you speak. "I made the top bit clearer" and "I lifted the shadow so the expression reads" describe the same edit, and only one of them sounds like somebody who took this course.

The terms most likely to come up: treatment, consistency, reading order, compositional mechanism, destination, specification statement, provenance, retention, unedited original.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. Which image in your set would you least like to be asked about? Prepare that one first.
  2. Your test said viewers went to the wrong place. How many of your other images have the same problem? (If you have not looked, that is this week's work — re-read section 1.)
  3. Did the revision change your treatment? How would you know?
  4. For each image: what is it doing that no other image in the set does?
  5. Is your walkthrough ordered by your argument, or by the order you shot things? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  6. Which image is in your campaign only because you happened to take it?

What happens in the session

Walkthroughs, across two sittings, with a running order published in advance. You go through your campaign image by image and answer questions — including about an image you did not choose to discuss.

Your final image set and the walkthrough together are Phase 6, submitted as the assignment.

The revision is not done in the session. Two sittings and the whole class means there is no editing time in the room. Arrive finished.
