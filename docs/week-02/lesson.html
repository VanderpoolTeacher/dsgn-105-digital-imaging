Week 02 · Where images come from — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Last week you built a file and found out it has a reader. This week is about everything that arrives before the file: where an image came from, what you are allowed to do to it, and what you are allowed to make from nothing.

You will photograph people in two weeks. The commitments you write this week are the ones you will be held to then.

1 · Provenance

Provenance is where an image came from, and what permission its use requires.

Last week you built a composition from a supplied element pack. Try to answer, right now, without looking: who made those images? Where were they taken? Could you use one on a poster that a nonprofit distributes to four thousand people?

Almost nobody can answer. That is not a failure of attention — it is the normal condition of images once they have been through two or three hands. And it is exactly the position your audience will be in relative to your campaign: they will see a photograph with no idea where it came from, and they will believe things about it accordingly.

Three questions settle provenance for anything you use:

  1. Who made it?
  2. What permission does this use require? Not permission in general — permission for the size, the audience, and the purpose you have in mind.
  3. What does the person in the photograph know about how it will be used?

The third one is not a legal question and it is the one that matters most in this course.

2 · Permission is specific

Copyright and fair use are real and you should know they exist. But the operative question in a campaign is narrower and more practical than either.

Not the question The question
"Is this image copyrighted?" "What does this use need, and do I have it?"
"Can I use images of people?" "Does this person know their photograph will be on a poster about food insecurity?"
"Is it fair use?" "Would the subject recognise themselves in how they have been used?"

A photograph you own outright, of a person who agreed to be photographed, can still be used in a way they never agreed to. Owning an image and being entitled to a particular use of it are different things.

Your campaign brief this week includes ethical commitments — what you will and will not do to make a picture stronger. Write them now, before you have a shoot you are trying to rescue.

3 · Manipulation that clarifies, and manipulation that misrepresents

Every photograph is manipulated. Framing is a choice about what to leave out; exposure is a choice about what to make visible. There is no unedited baseline to fall back on, so "don't manipulate images" is not a usable rule.

The usable distinction is what the manipulation does to a viewer's understanding.

Clarifying manipulation removes something that was getting in the way of what the photograph is actually about. Dust on the lens. A colour cast from bad lighting. A distracting bright patch in a corner.

Misrepresenting manipulation changes what a viewer would reasonably conclude from the image.

Clarifies Misrepresents
Removing a dust spot from the sky Removing the litter that showed the site was neglected
Correcting a green cast from fluorescent lighting Warming a face to make somebody look healthier than they were
Cropping out the edge of a tripod leg Cropping out the queue that showed how many people were waiting
Brightening shadows so a face is legible Brightening a room so it looks better funded than it is

The technique is identical in every pair. Same tool, same skill, same number of clicks. Nothing in the craft tells you which column you are in — which is why this cannot be assessed by looking at how well something was done.

The test is a question about the viewer, not about the file:

Would somebody conclude something from this image that is not true?

4 · The invisible problem

The manipulations above are all things you can look for. The harder case is an image with nothing visibly wrong with it at all.

An image can be technically flawless, beautifully made, and still a problem:

  • It is not what it claims to be — a stock photograph presented as this organization's own work.
  • Its source is undisclosed, so a viewer cannot weigh it.
  • It uses its subject rather than serving them — a person photographed at their worst moment to make somebody else feel something.
  • It generalises from one person to a group in a way that person never agreed to.

None of these announce themselves. You cannot find them by looking harder at the pixels; you find them by asking what the image is doing, and for whom.

This is what the session assesses. In class you will look at images you did not make and say what, if anything, is wrong with them. Some of them are fine. At least one has a problem you will not see by examining it.

Catching a flaw that announces itself shows you were looking. Catching one that does not shows you were evaluating.

5 · Generative AI, and where the line is

Generative AI produces image content from a written input. In this course it is required — outcome 6 of the six says so — and it is bounded.

Required Not allowed
Exploring visual directions Producing the source photographs you are assessed on
Remixing and recombining existing imagery Generating a "photograph" of your subject
Enhancing images you made Standing in for a shoot you did not do

The reason for the boundary is the assessment, not a position on AI. Outcome 4 asks you to edit and retouch photographs — and what makes that judgeable is comparing your edit against your own original. Generate the original and there is nothing to compare, and the outcome cannot be marked at all.

You will also meet the other side of this line in DSGN 107 and in COMM 260, where the rules differ because the outcomes differ. That is not inconsistency; each course draws the line where its own evidence requires it.

6 · Retention, and why intent is invisible without it

Here is the part that costs people marks.

When you generate, keep everything. Every output, including the ones you would rather nobody saw. Number them in the order they came out, and write one line for each one you reject: why it was wrong for this intent.

The reason is that what you kept proves nothing. A good final image is equally consistent with two stories — you knew what you wanted and worked toward it, or you accepted the first thing the tool produced and it happened to be good.

The only place your intention is visible is the gap between what you asked for and what you kept.

Submitted What it shows
Four kept images You can operate a tool
Four kept, eleven rejected, each with a reason You had an intention and pursued it
Rejections all reading "didn't like it" You had reactions, not an intention

Deleting a bad output is a reflex. Every tool you have ever used treats a failure as something to throw away. In this course the failures are evidence, and once they are gone they cannot be reconstructed — not by you, not by me, not with any amount of good will.

Words you will hear but do not own yet

  • Critique — observed evaluation of images you did not make, across clarity, ethical use, and appropriateness. Practised this week; owned by weeks 4–7, where four sessions carry the whole of outcome 5.
  • Treatment, consistency — mentioned when we talk about a campaign holding together. Owned and assessed in week 6.

Practise

Do the clarify-or-misrepresent interactive before the session. Six pairs, three rounds. Unscored — it exists so the judgment is not brand new when it counts.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. Where did the images in last week's element pack come from? What would you need to find out before using one publicly?
  2. Removing a dust spot and removing a piece of litter are the same edit. What makes them different?
  3. A photograph is technically perfect and beautifully lit. Name three ways it could still be a problem. (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  4. Why may generative AI not produce your campaign photographs — and which outcome is the reason?
  5. You generate twelve images and keep three. What do the other nine prove, and what happens if you delete them? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 6.)
  6. What are the three questions that settle provenance?

What happens in the session

You will see one photograph edited two ways — identical craft, opposite relationships to the truth. Then in the lab you generate against a stated intent, keep everything, and work through a set of images you did not make.

Afterwards you submit the campaign brief — Phase 1 — as the assignment.
