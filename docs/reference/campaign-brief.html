Awareness Campaign — Student Brief

Step 3a of the Course Build Process · student-facing

What you're making

A photo-based awareness campaign for a local nonprofit — five to seven images that hold together as one piece of work and say something the organization has struggled to say in words. You will take the photographs, edit them, and deliver them ready for two destinations: printed, and on a screen.

Why this exists

Almost anything you make after this course will be an image that has to work somewhere specific, for someone who is not you. This project is where you stop asking whether a picture looks good and start asking whether it lands — with a real audience, at a real size, on real paper or a real screen. The habits are the point: state what you meant, make choices that serve it, and find out from someone else whether it arrived.

How this breaks down

Phase 1 — Find the gap

Goal: Understand what the organization needs people to know, and why words have not done it.

Do this:

  1. Pick your organization. Take one from the shortlist you were given, or propose your own local nonprofit for approval. Either way, you also receive a written brief for that organization — you are never blocked waiting for a reply.
  2. Read everything the organization already puts out: website, socials, printed material, annual report if there is one.
  3. Write down what the images they currently use are doing, and what they are not doing.
  4. Name the audience. Not "the community" — a person, where they are, and what they already believe.
  5. Write your ethical commitments before you photograph anyone: who appears, what permission you need, and what you will not do to make a picture stronger.

Hand in: Campaign brief, one document.

You're done when: The brief names a gap specific enough that someone else could tell whether a given image closes it. "Raise awareness" is not a gap. "People assume the food pantry serves people who are not working" is.

Watch out for: Writing the brief about the organization instead of about the audience. The organization is not who needs convincing.

Phase 2 — Explore three directions

Goal: Find out what the campaign could look like before committing to what it will look like.

Do this:

  1. Build three visually distinct directions — different treatment, tone, and approach to composition. Distinct means someone could tell them apart across a room.
  2. Use a generative AI tool to explore each direction. Keep everything it produces. The ones you throw away are part of what you hand in.
  3. For each rejected output, write one line: why it was wrong for this campaign.
  4. Write what you were trying to get the tool to do, and what you decided about what it gave you — including anything that stereotyped or misrepresented the people this organization serves.
  5. Choose one direction and say why the other two lost.

Hand in: Three directions, all generated material kept and rejected, intent and ethics statement.

You're done when: Someone reading your rejects can see what you were after before they read your statement. If you kept the first thing the tool made, you have shown that you can operate a tool, not that you had an intention.

Watch out for: Three directions that are the same idea in three colour schemes.

Phase 3 — Shoot and edit

Goal: Make the campaign images.

Do this:

  1. Shoot. These are your photographs — AI may not make them for you. Shoot more than you need.
  2. Back up your originals untouched before you edit anything. You hand these in.
  3. Edit five to seven images toward your chosen direction.
  4. Work non-destructively — layers, masks, adjustment layers. Your working file has to show how the image was built.
  5. Apply the same treatment across all of them. Look at them side by side, not one at a time.

Hand in: Working files, exports, and the unedited originals.

You're done when: Your before-and-after pairs show the edit changed what the photograph says, not only how clean it is. And the set reads as one campaign — a stranger could tell these belong together.

Watch out for: Editing each image until it is good on its own. Seven individually excellent images with seven different treatments is not a campaign.

Phase 4 — Take one image to two destinations

Goal: Deliver the same image correctly to print and to screen.

Do this:

  1. Pick one campaign image. Choose your two destinations specifically — not "print" but "A3 poster on a corridor wall"; not "digital" but "Instagram feed post".
  2. From the one working file, produce an export for each.
  3. For each export write down: pixel dimensions and resolution, file format, colour system — and why each, for that destination.
  4. Check the files you are handing in actually match what you wrote.

Hand in: Two exports and two specification statements.

You're done when: The two specifications differ, and each difference is explained by what that destination needs. Identical settings for a printed poster and a phone screen means you used a default.

Watch out for: Writing the numbers you think are correct rather than the numbers in the file. These get checked against each other.

Phase 5 — Find out what people actually see

Goal: Test whether your composition produces the reading order you intended.

Do this:

  1. Before showing anyone: write down what a viewer should notice first, second, third in your lead image.
  2. Write down which choices you made to produce that order — size, contrast, placement, direction, isolation.
  3. Show the image to viewers who have not been told your intent. Ask what they noticed first, then next.
  4. Record what they actually said, including the answers you did not want.
  5. Revise the image, or explain why you are not going to.

Hand in: Intended order, reported orders, and your revision.

You're done when: You can point at the specific compositional choice that produces the order — not the subject matter. If the eye lands first on the only face in the frame, the photograph arranged that, not you.

Watch out for: Telling viewers what to look at before asking them what they saw. That destroys the only measurement you get.

Phase 6 — Finish and account for it

Goal: Deliver the campaign and walk someone through it.

Do this:

  1. Apply what Phase 5 told you across the whole set, not just the tested image.
  2. Check consistency one last time with all images open together.
  3. Build a walkthrough: image by image, what you meant and what you did.
  4. Be ready to be asked about a decision you did not prepare for.

Hand in: Final image set and the live walkthrough.

You're done when: The set is consistent, the revisions are visible, and you can account for any image in it.

Watch out for: Revising only the image you tested. The test tells you something about your habits, not about one photograph.

Running throughout — Critique

Critique is not a phase. It happens repeatedly across the term, on other people's images, and it is scored on its own rubric.

Each time, you cover three things: whether the image is clear, whether it is ethically sound, and whether it fits where it is going. Every session you leave a written record of what you said.

The hardest and most valuable thing you can do here is raise a problem with an image that has nothing visibly wrong with it — a manipulation that misleads, a source nobody disclosed, a subject who is being used rather than served. Spotting a flaw that announces itself shows you were looking. Spotting one that does not shows you were evaluating.

The walkthrough

Live, image by image. For each: what you intended, what you did to get it, and how it serves the campaign. Expect questions, including about images you did not plan to dwell on.

It is worth 5% and it does not carry the project. A confident walkthrough over a weak campaign scores exactly what the campaign scores, plus five.

How you'll be graded

Scored on two separate rubrics — the project rubric and the critique rubric.

Component Weight
Campaign brief 10%
Direction study 15%
Campaign image set 25%
Output package 15%
Reading-order test 15%
Critique record 15%
Campaign walkthrough 5%

The brief and the walkthrough together are 15%. Everything else is the work itself. Polish on the packaging cannot carry thin images — the arithmetic does not allow it.

Disclosure

Every submission carries a statement declaring:

  • Source material — which photographs are yours, and where anything else came from.
  • AI assistance — the tools, what you asked for, what came back, what you kept, what you rejected, and what you did to it afterwards.

Generative AI is required in this project. Use it for ideation, remixing, compositing, and enhancement. It may not produce the source photographs — those are yours, and they are what your editing is judged against.

This is not only a policy. Without your originals there is nothing to compare your retouching to, and without your rejects there is no way to tell an intention from a lucky first result. An undisclosed source leaves two parts of this project with nothing to grade.
