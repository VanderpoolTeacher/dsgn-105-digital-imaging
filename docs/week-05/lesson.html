Week 05 · Isolating and combining — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Last week you found out that you cannot judge your own composition. This week the same problem appears in a different form: you cannot judge your own edges at the size you have been looking at them.

1 · Everything looks fine at fit-to-screen

You work at whatever zoom shows the whole image. So does everybody. At that size a cut-out looks clean, a mask looks convincing, and two sources look like one photograph.

None of that survives 100%.

100% means one image pixel per screen pixel — the actual data, not a preview. It is the size a printed poster is looked at from close range, and it is the size any professional looks at work before accepting it.

The gap between those two views is where this week's marks live. A student who never leaves fit-to-screen is not checking their work; they are looking at a thumbnail of it.

2 · Selection tools are mostly wrong

Every selection tool is wrong for most subjects and right for a few. The skill is not learning the tools — it is asking what kind of edge is this?

Edge What works What fails
Hard, high contrast — a sign, a box Quick selection, magic wand Nothing much; this is the easy case
Soft — hair, fur, foliage Refined edge tools, channels, patience Any hard-edged selection. It will look cut out because it was
Semi-transparent — glass, smoke, water Blending and masking rather than selecting Selection of any kind. There is no line to find
Motion-blurred Feathered masking, accepting some background Precision tools, which produce a sharp edge around a soft thing

The test for 5.1 is whether the edge survives a new background, and that is a property of the choice rather than of the tool. A hair selection that looks perfect against the original grey wall will show every mistake against a dark sky.

3 · Masks, not deletions

You met this argument in week 1 as adjustment layers. Here it is again at higher stakes.

Deleting a selection is final. The pixels are gone. If the edge is wrong — and the first edge is always wrong somewhere — you start again.

Masking hides instead of deleting. The pixels are still there, hidden by a mask you can paint back into. Wrong edge? Paint some back. Too much hair removed? Paint it back.

Delete Mask
Fixing a mistake Start again Paint
Refining over time Not possible Normal
What the file records The result The decision
Visible in the export No No

That last row is why this is graded on the working file rather than on the exported image. A deleted selection and a masked one produce an identical picture. The difference exists only in the file, and only until somebody flattens it.

5.2 asks for a mask that can be revised rather than redone — which is why the lab requires you to actually revise one. A mask that happened to be right first time demonstrates a lucky selection, not revisability.

4 · Smart objects, briefly

Scale an image down, then scale it back up, and you have lost detail permanently — the software threw pixels away at step one and invented them at step two.

A smart object keeps the original data and applies your transformations as instructions. Scale it down and up as often as you like; the original is untouched.

The thing to remember: damage compounds. Three destructive transformations are worse than one, and each one is invisible on its own.

5 · The join

A composite fails where two sources meet. Most students look only at the edge, and the edge is only half of it.

The edge — is it hard where it should be soft, haloed, or fringed with colour from the original background?

The match — do the two sources agree about:

The question
Light direction Is your subject lit from the left and your background from the right?
Light quality Hard sun on the subject, overcast on the background?
Colour temperature Warm indoor light dropped into cool daylight?
Grain and sharpness A clean modern file on a grainy scan, or a sharp subject on a soft background?

A perfectly cut subject with the wrong light still reads as two photographs. Students see the edge because they worked on it for an hour, and stop seeing the whole.

5.3 asks whether the join announces itself — a whole-image judgment, not an edge one.

Module 3 vocabulary

Term Means
Selection A defined region of an image, made in order to act on it
Mask A non-destructive means of hiding part of a layer without deleting it
Composite One image built from more than one source
Join Where two composited sources meet. Judged at 100%, never at fit-to-screen

Words you will hear but do not own yet

  • Treatment, consistency — how a set of images holds together. Owned and assessed in week 6.
  • Sequence — the order images are arranged in. Owned in week 7.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. What does 100% actually mean, and why is it the size that matters?
  2. You have selected hair against a grey wall and it looks perfect. What happens when you put it on a dark sky? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 2.)
  3. Name two things a mask lets you do that a deleted selection does not.
  4. Why is this assessed on the working file rather than on the exported image?
  5. Your cut-out is flawless and the composite still looks wrong. Name three things to check that are not the edge. (If this is not obvious, re-read section 5.)
  6. What is a smart object for?

What happens in the session

You will watch a composite that looks entirely convincing fall apart when it is zoomed to full size. Then in the lab you composite the hard subject you brought, revise your mask at least once, and check the join at 100% — twice, once yourself and once by a partner on a different machine.

Campaign editing also begins, and critique session 2 runs. Afterwards you submit the composite as the assignment, which is 10% of your course grade.
