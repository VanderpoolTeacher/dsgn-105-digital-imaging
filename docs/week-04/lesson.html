Week 04 · What the eye does first — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

You have built files, judged other people's images, and learned what makes a file arrive. This week you start making images of your own — and the first thing to establish is that you cannot tell whether they work.

1 · Visual hierarchy is a claim about somebody else

Visual hierarchy is the order in which elements of an image are noticed. First, second, third.

Here is the difficulty. You made the image. You know where the important thing is, you have been looking at it for an hour, and your eye goes there immediately — not because the composition sends it there, but because you already know. Your own reading of your own image is worthless as evidence.

So is the instructor's agreement that it looks well arranged. So is a classmate's, if you told them what you were going for first.

The only instrument that works is somebody who does not already know, looking at it once, and reporting what happened.

This is the conceptual turn of the week and students resist it, because it means the thing you are most confident about is the thing you are least able to check.

2 · The five mechanisms

An image directs attention through a small number of causes. Learn them as causes, not as a list.

Mechanism What it does
Scale Bigger things are noticed first — but only relative to what is around them
Contrast The lightest thing on a dark ground, or the one saturated colour in a muted frame
Placement Where in the frame, and what it is aligned with or set apart from
Direction Lines, edges, and gazes that point. A face looking left drags the eye left
Isolation Space around a thing. One object with room around it beats five crowded ones

Isolation is the most underused and the most reliable. Students reach for scale and contrast first because those are the loudest. Space is quieter and it works better.

3 · The subject-matter trap

Here is the failure this week is built to catch.

You photograph a room. There is one person in it. You ask a viewer what they noticed first and they say "the person". You write down that your composition directed the eye to the person.

It did not. The world did. A face in a frame of objects is noticed first whatever you do; you did not arrange that, and if you had put the person anywhere else in the frame the answer would have been the same.

Not evidence of hierarchy Evidence of hierarchy
The eye went to the only face The eye went to the left-hand face, because it is lit and the other is not
The eye went to the only text The eye went to the text because it sits in the one uncluttered area
The eye went to the brightest thing The eye went to the brightest thing and you made it the brightest thing

The test is always: could I have changed this? If moving, resizing, lighting or spacing something would have changed the answer, you arranged it. If the answer would have been the same regardless, the subject matter supplied it.

4 · How to ask without telling

A reading-order test is easy to run and easy to invalidate. The invalidation is almost always accidental and almost always in the first sentence.

Leading means telling the viewer what to look at before asking what they saw. It feels helpful — you are giving them context — and it destroys the measurement, because they will then report the thing you named.

Leading Not leading
"This is about the volunteer — where does your eye go?" "What do you notice first?"
"Do you see the sign at the back?" "And after that?"
"Does the woman stand out?" "Anything else?"
"I was trying to draw you to the left. Did it work?" (ask nothing until they have answered)

Write your questions down before you ask them. That is not bureaucracy — it is the only way to know afterwards what you actually said, and this week you are graded on the wording rather than on the result.

Three more rules that matter:

  • Ask people from outside this class. Classmates know the vocabulary and often know the image.
  • Ask at least two people. Two viewers who disagree is a real result, not a failed test.
  • Write down the answers you did not want. Those are the ones with information in them.

5 · What to do when the test contradicts you

Your viewers report an order that is not the one you intended. This is the normal outcome, especially the first time.

The wrong response is to explain. The viewer is not mistaken — they read the image the way the image reads.

The right response is to ask which mechanism produced what they actually saw, and then change it:

  • They went to the bright corner first? Contrast is doing something you did not intend. Darken it.
  • They never found your subject? It probably has nothing around it — try isolation before you try making it bigger.
  • They read right-to-left through your image? Something has direction you did not notice. A gaze, an edge, a line.

Then apply the change everywhere it applies, not only to the image you tested. The test tells you something about how you compose, not about one photograph.

Module 3 vocabulary

Term Means
Composition The deliberate arrangement of elements within a frame
Visual hierarchy The order in which elements are noticed. A property of how an image is read, not of how it was made
Reading order The sequence a viewer actually notices things in — first, second, third
Reading-order test Asking viewers who have not been told the intent what they noticed, in order
Leading Telling a viewer what to look at before asking what they saw. Invalidates the test
Compositional mechanism The specific choice producing the reading order — scale, contrast, placement, direction, or isolation
Isolation Separating an element from its surroundings so it is noticed first

Words you will hear but do not own yet

  • Selection, composite, join — mentioned when we talk about what happens to your photographs next. Owned and assessed in week 5.
  • Treatment, consistency — how a set holds together. Owned and assessed in week 6.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. Name the five mechanisms without looking.
  2. A viewer says they noticed the only person in the photograph first. What have you learnt about your composition? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 3.)
  3. Why can't you assess the reading order of your own image?
  4. Rewrite this so it does not lead: "I wanted your eye to go to the poster on the wall — did it?"
  5. Your two viewers report different orders. Is the test broken? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  6. Which mechanism would you try first for something that is being missed entirely, and why?

What happens in the session

You will hear the same image asked about two ways, once leading and once not, and hear the difference the question makes. Then in the lab you run a real test on an image you did not make, start shooting your campaign, and take part in the first critique session.

Afterwards you submit the test — including the exact questions you asked — as the assignment.
