Week 03 · The numbers that decide whether a file arrives — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

The last two weeks were about how a file is built and where images come from. This week is about whether the file arrives — whether what you made survives being printed at A3, or posted to a feed, or sent to somebody who will open it on a phone.

This is the least glamorous week in the course and the one that most often decides whether professional work is usable.

1 · Pixel dimensions are the only real measurement

A raster image has a width and a height in pixels. That is the whole of what exists in the file.

Resolution does not exist until you name a physical size. An image is not "72 dpi" the way it is 3000 pixels wide. Resolution is the answer to a question — how many pixels per inch when this is printed at this size — and until somebody names the size, the question has not been asked.

This inversion is the most useful idea of the week, and almost everything else follows from it.

What students usually believe What is actually true
The image "is" 72 dpi The image is 3000 × 2000 pixels. It becomes 72 ppi if you print it 41 inches wide
Increasing resolution improves the image Increasing resolution without adding pixels just prints it smaller
A "high-res" image is a property you can request Pixel dimensions are the property. Resolution is a plan for them

2 · ppi and dpi

Two abbreviations, used interchangeably everywhere, meaning different things.

ppi — pixels per inch. A property of an image prepared for a physical size. Yours to decide.

dpi — dots per inch. A property of a printing device — how finely the machine can place ink. Not yours; the printer's.

You will see "300 dpi" used to mean "a good print image" for the rest of your career. It is worth knowing that the phrase is wrong and what it is trying to say: enough pixels that, at the size I am printing, there are about 300 of them per inch.

3 · What "enough pixels" means

A useful rule of thumb, and it is a rule of thumb rather than a law:

Pixels needed = printed size in inches × target ppi

An A3 poster is roughly 11.7 × 16.5 inches. At 300 ppi that needs about 3510 × 4950 pixels.

Now look at the three numbers you measured before class. A recent phone photograph is comfortably above that. A web image is nowhere near. A screenshot is somewhere in between and depends entirely on the screen it came from.

The image cannot be rescued by asking for more resolution. Setting a small image to 300 ppi does not add pixels — the software simply prints it smaller, or invents the missing ones, which is what makes an enlarged photograph look soft.

4 · File formats are a set of costs

There is no best format. Every one gives something up, and choosing well means knowing what you are willing to lose.

Format Gives you Costs you
PSD Layers, masks, everything editable Enormous; nobody outside the studio can open it
TIFF Lossless quality, print-ready, wide support Very large files
JPG Small files, universal Compression artefacts that worsen every time you re-save. No transparency
PNG Lossless, transparency Large for photographs; poor fit for print
PDF Reliable for print, holds colour and type Not an editing format; awkward for screen posting

The question to ask is never "which format is best". It is "what am I willing to lose here, for this destination?"

5 · Colour systems, and what cannot be recovered

RGB builds colour from light — red, green, blue. Screens work this way. It can express very saturated colours.

CMYK builds colour from ink — cyan, magenta, yellow, black. Print works this way. It cannot express some of the colours RGB can.

Gamut is the range of colours a system can express. RGB's is larger than CMYK's, and the difference is the part that matters:

Converting RGB to CMYK loses colours that have no ink equivalent. Converting back does not bring them back.

The bright saturated blue that looked electric on your screen becomes a duller blue in print, and if you then convert that back to RGB you get the duller blue, not the original. The conversion is one-way in practice, which is why you keep your working file in the colour system you are editing in and convert on export — not before.

6 · The check

Exporting is not the last step. Opening the exported file and reading its actual numbers is.

It takes ten seconds when it is a habit and it never happens when it is not. It is also, this week, exactly what you are graded on: the specification you write and the file you deliver either agree or they do not, and that is checkable without anybody's opinion.

The common failure is writing the statement from the export dialogue rather than from the file. The dialogue shows what you asked for. The file shows what happened. On this outcome those are different things.

Module 2 vocabulary

Term Means
Destination The specific place an image is going — a stated print size and stock, or a stated platform and placement. Never "print" or "digital" alone
Specification statement The written record of pixel dimensions and resolution, file format, and colour system chosen for one destination, with the reason for each
Pixel dimensions The width and height of an image in pixels. The only measure that exists in the file itself
Resolution Pixels per unit of physical output. Meaningless without a stated physical size
ppi Pixels per inch — a property of an image prepared for a physical size
dpi Dots per inch — a property of a printing device, not of an image file
File format The container an image is written to, each with a different cost
Colour system The model describing how colour values are stored — RGB, CMYK, Lab
Gamut The range of colours a system can express. What is lost moving RGB to CMYK
Export Writing a file for a destination from a working file. The working file is never the delivery

Words you will hear but do not own yet

  • Treatment, consistency — mentioned when we talk about a set of images holding together. Owned and assessed in week 6.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. An image is 1200 × 800 pixels. What is its resolution?
  2. You need an A3 poster at 300 ppi. Roughly how many pixels do you need, and does your phone photograph have them? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 3.)
  3. What is the difference between ppi and dpi, and which one is yours to decide?
  4. Name one thing JPG gives you and one thing it costs you.
  5. You convert an image to CMYK, then back to RGB. What did you get back? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 5.)
  6. Why is "print" not a destination?

What happens in the session

You will hold two prints of the same image made at different resolutions — the one argument in this week that a screen cannot make. Then in the lab you take one supplied image to two named destinations and write a specification statement for each.

Afterwards you submit both exports and both statements as the assignment, which is 10% of your course grade.
