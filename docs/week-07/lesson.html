Week 07 · Meeting a viewer who was not told — Lesson

DSGN 105 · Digital Imaging

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

This is the heaviest week of the course. Two things you rehearsed weeks ago come back at full stakes on the same day, and one new idea arrives — how the order of your images changes what they say.

Read the last section on planning before you do anything else.

1 · Sequence

Your set holds together now. This week it acquires an order, and the order says something the individual images do not.

A sequence is an arrangement where moving an image changes what the set means. That is the test: if you could shuffle your seven photographs without loss, you have a folder rather than a sequence.

Some things order does:

Establishes then complicates A wide view sets the place; the close portrait that follows lands differently than it would alone
Builds a rhythm Two quiet images then a loud one. The loud one is loud because of what preceded it
Answers a question it raised An image that withholds something, followed by the one that gives it
Ends deliberately The last image is the one the viewer leaves with, and that is a decision

The strongest test is to swap your first and last image and read the set again. If nothing changes, the order is not doing work yet.

2 · The output package — week 3, for real

You did this in week 3 on a supplied file. Now it is your own image, your own destinations, and it is 15% of your campaign.

The procedure has not changed:

  1. Name both destinations concretely — a print size and stock, a platform and placement.
  2. Export both from one working file.
  3. Write a specification statement for each: pixel dimensions and resolution, file format, colour system, and why, for that destination.
  4. Reopen both delivered files and read the numbers off the files. Reconcile against what you wrote.

Two things are different this time.

You have to actually print it. A print specification for a print that was never printed has not been checked, and printing has a queue. This is why the lab runs printing first.

The image is one you care about, which introduces a failure that did not exist in week 3: choosing settings that flatter the image rather than serve the destination. If your reason for a setting is "it looks better", that is a preference and not a specification.

3 · The reading-order test — week 4, for real

Also familiar, also higher stakes: 15% of your campaign, and this time it is your own lead image.

The procedure is the same. Write the intended order and the mechanisms. Write the exact questions. Test on at least two viewers from outside the class. Record what they said, verbatim, including what you did not want.

The difficulty is new. In week 4 you did not care what the answer was. Now you do, and that changes two things:

The urge to explain is much stronger. You will want to say what the campaign is about before you show it. That single sentence destroys the measurement, and it will feel like helpfulness rather than sabotage.

A contradicting result feels like a verdict on you. It is not. It is information about how you compose, and it is the most useful thing you will get this week.

4 · What 7.3 actually asks

Not that you run the test. That you act on a result that contradicts your intent.

Response What it demonstrates
The viewer was distracted; run it again until it agrees Nothing. You have found somebody who confirms you
Explain why the intended order is correct Nothing. The image reads how it reads
Identify which mechanism produced what they actually saw, and change it 7.3
Change it, then check whether the same habit shows up elsewhere in the set 7.3, and week 8's outcome as well

The last row matters. The test tells you something about how you compose, not about one photograph. Applying the change only to the image you tested is week 8's most common failure, and it starts here.

5 · Planning this week

Blunt version: two phases are due, each is the sole evidence for an outcome, the last critique session runs, and the course evaluation period takes class time.

The order is not optional:

  1. Print first. It has a queue, and a failed print discovered on the last day is a failed component.
  2. Then the reading-order test — it needs two people from outside the class, which needs finding them.
  3. Then the sequence — the only thing here with no external dependency.

Anything you leave until the end of this week is a thing you may not get to do at all.

Check yourself before the session

Not graded. Two of these matter more than the others.

  1. Swap the first and last image in your set. Did anything change?
  2. Why does the lab run printing before the reading-order tests? (If this is not obvious, re-read section 5.)
  3. What is the difference between a specification statement and a description of your settings?
  4. Your viewer reports an order that is not the one you wanted. Name the two responses that demonstrate nothing. (If this is not obvious, re-read section 4.)
  5. You want a setting because the image looks better with it. Is that a reason?
  6. What does 7.3 ask you to do beyond running the test?

What happens in the session

You will see one set of photographs arranged two ways, saying two different things. Then in the lab you build and print your output package, run the reading-order test on your lead image with viewers from outside the class, and take part in the last critique session of the course.

Afterwards you submit Phase 4 and Phase 5 as the assignment — 30% of your campaign between them.
